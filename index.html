§ 00 · Abstract

Abstract

Reinforcement learning policies trained for legged locomotion achieve high task performance but produce high-frequency, jittery motor commands that accelerate mechanical wear, increase energy consumption, and destabilise contact forces. We treat this as an experimental investigation: given a frozen, jittery PPO policy, what is the most effective way to reduce its action jitter while preserving — or improving — its task performance? We compare three families of jitter-reduction techniques: reward-side action-rate regularisation during PPO training, online causal low-pass filtering at deployment, and a learned post-hoc action refinement based on conditional flow matching. We benchmark these methods individually and in combination through a 9-variant ablation crossing three reward regularisation levels with four post-hoc smoothing schemes, evaluated on a Unitree Go1 in NVIDIA Isaac Sim under 80-environment push-recovery stress tests with 50–500 N lateral impulses. The best configuration — moderate reward regularisation paired with a flow-matched refinement trained on physics-aware optimal targets — achieves a 46.25 percentage-point reduction in fall rate (50.00% → 3.75%) and −38% high-frequency spectral energy, while adding only ~6 ms of inference overhead per 20 ms control step. The ablation reveals a sharp interaction: removing reward regularisation weakens the flow's benefit, while pushing it too far breaks the policy so completely that the learned refinement collapses to total failure.

§ 01 · Background

Background

Why are RL policies jittery?

A trained PPO policy [2] outputs $a_t = \mu_\theta(s_t)$. Because the policy network is locally Lipschitz in its observation, small state perturbations get amplified through its Jacobian:

$$\Delta a_t \approx J_{\mu}(s_{t-1}) \Delta s_t.$$

The PPO objective rewards task performance via the clipped surrogate

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min\!\left( r_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon) \hat{A}_t \right) \right],$$

but contains no explicit incentive for smooth actuator commands. On the Unitree Go1's high-gain PD controller ($K_p{=}25,\, K_d{=}0.5$), discontinuous action jumps map directly into torque spikes every 20 ms, producing fluctuating ground reaction forces that destabilise contact. The downstream costs are real: gearbox wear, 20–40% higher instantaneous power draw, audible noise, reduced sim-to-real transfer success, and degraded recovery from disturbances.

Three families of jitter-reduction methods

We study three approaches that intervene at different stages of the policy lifecycle:

(A) Reward-side regularisation. Add a penalty $\lambda \lVert a_t - a_{t-1} \rVert^2$ directly to the PPO reward, training the policy to prefer smooth actions. This is the IsaacLab default at $\lambda{=}{-}0.01$. It is conceptually clean — smoothness becomes a primary objective — but entangles smoothness with task performance during training, requires retraining whenever the smoothness target changes, and as we will show has a sharp sweet spot.

(B) Online filtering. Apply a causal first-order Butterworth IIR low-pass to actions at deployment time, $y_n = (1{-}\alpha) y_{n-1} + \alpha x_n$ with cutoff $f_c{=}15$ Hz. Cheap and immediate, but task-blind: the filter cannot distinguish between meaningless oscillation and a legitimate rapid corrective action.

(C) Learned post-hoc refinement. Train a generative model offline to map raw policy actions toward task-aware "optimal" targets, applied as a post-processing step at deployment. This is where flow matching comes in. It is computationally heavier than filtering but, in principle, can produce corrections that are both smooth and physics-aware.

We are particularly interested in the interaction between these methods. Are they substitutes or complements? Does combining them yield super-additive gains, or do they undo each other? The 9-variant ablation in this report is designed to answer exactly that.

§ 02 · Flow Matching

A Brief Introduction to Flow Matching

Of the three jitter-reduction methods studied, flow matching is the most novel and warrants explanation. Flow matching [1] is a simulation-free training procedure for continuous normalising flows that turns generative modelling into a clean supervised regression problem.

From distributions to velocity fields

Suppose we want to transport samples from a source distribution $q_0$ to a target $q_1$. A continuous normalising flow defines a time-dependent vector field $u_t(x)$ whose flow $\phi_t$ is the solution of the ODE

$$\frac{d}{dt} \phi_t(x_0) = u_t(\phi_t(x_0)),\qquad \phi_0(x_0) = x_0,$$

and whose induced probability path $p_t$ interpolates $p_0 = q_0$ and $p_1 = q_1$. The continuity equation links the two: $\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (u_t \, p_t)$. The naive flow-matching loss would regress a network $u_\theta$ against the marginal vector field,

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1],\; x \sim p_t} \left\lVert u_\theta(t, x) - u_t(x) \right\rVert^2,$$

but the marginal $u_t(x)$ is intractable. The Cambridge MLG exposition [5] walks through the resolution clearly.

Conditional flow matching

Lipman et al. [1] show that conditioning on the endpoints $z = (x_0, x_1)$ — a two-sided conditioning — gives a tractable surrogate loss with the same gradient. The conditional flow matching objective is

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\; (x_0, x_1) \sim q,\; x_t \sim p_t(\cdot \mid x_0, x_1)} \left\lVert u_\theta(t, x_t) - u_t(x_t \mid x_0, x_1) \right\rVert^2,$$(CFM)

and remarkably $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}$. Training on the tractable conditional loss yields a network that learns the intractable marginal field [1].

Optimal transport paths

The choice of conditional path matters. With a deterministic linear interpolation

$$x_t = (1-t)\, x_0 + t\, x_1,\qquad u_t(x_t \mid x_0, x_1) = x_1 - x_0,$$(OT path)

the conditional vector field is constant along the path and equal to the displacement $x_1 - x_0$ — the optimal transport map under squared-Euclidean cost. This is the path we use throughout. Its key practical property is that learned marginal trajectories tend to be near-straight, which dramatically reduces the number of Euler integration steps needed at inference compared to diffusion models [6].

Why flow matching for jitter reduction?

Three properties make flow matching well-suited to this problem. First, the regression target $x_1 - x_0$ is fixed and simple — the loss is a clean MSE against a constant displacement, producing stable training without the adversarial or score-matching instabilities of GANs and diffusion models. Second, the loss supervises $u_\theta$ at every $t \in [0,1]$ rather than just at the endpoint, acting as an implicit temporal regulariser against noisy targets. This matters because our optimal targets — described next — are computed by argmin over a finite candidate set and are themselves noisy. Third, inference via Euler integration with early stopping at $t_{\text{end}} \in [0,1]$ provides a continuous deployment-time intensity dial: $t_{\text{end}}{=}0$ recovers the raw policy, $t_{\text{end}}{=}1$ applies full correction, intermediate values blend smoothly. None of this is possible with a one-shot supervised regressor.

§ 03 · Method

Method

Environment and base policies

The Unitree Go1 is a 12-DOF quadruped (3 joints per leg). Action space is 12-D joint position offsets from the default stance; observations are 48-D proprioceptive (base linear/angular velocities, projected gravity, velocity commands, joint positions, joint velocities, previous actions). Sim runs at 200 Hz with decimation-4 → 50 Hz control. We train PPO for 300 iterations with 4096 parallel envs in NVIDIA Isaac Lab [3], MLP[128,128,128] ELU actor and critic, GAE($\lambda{=}0.95$), discount $\gamma{=}0.99$, adaptive KL learning-rate schedule. The task is constant forward velocity tracking $v_x = 1.0$ m/s on flat terrain. We train three PPO policies that differ only in the action-rate reward weight $\lambda_{\text{a-rate}} \in \{0,\, {-}0.01,\, {-}0.5\}$, denoted no-reg, some-reg, extreme-reg.

Optimal target generation

For each timestep in a 5000-step PPO rollout, we restore the full simulator state into $K{=}16$ parallel environments and generate $K$ candidate actions: candidate 0 is exactly $a_{\text{ppo}}$ (guaranteeing $a^*$ is never worse in cost than the policy's own choice), candidates 1..15 are $a_{\text{ppo}} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ with $\sigma{=}0.1$. Each candidate is rolled out for $H{=}10$ steps with the PPO policy in the loop, accumulating a multi-objective cost

$$a^*_t = \arg\min_a \left( \lambda_1 \cdot \text{tracking} + \lambda_2 \lVert a - a_{t-1} \rVert^2 + \lambda_3 \cdot \text{energy} + \lambda_4 \cdot \text{instability} \right).$$

Tracking is $\lVert v_x - v_{\text{cmd}} \rVert^2$, energy is $\sum_i |\tau_i \dot q_i|$, and instability is $\lVert g_z + 1 \rVert^2$ on the projected gravity z-component (penalising tilt), with a 100.0 fall penalty for terminations. We use $\lambda_1{=}3, \lambda_2{=}0.5, \lambda_3{=}0.3, \lambda_4{=}2$ — the "balanced" weights settled on after early experiments with $\lambda_4{=}5$ produced overly cautious targets that barely moved.

The resulting $a^*$ trajectory is then Butterworth low-pass filtered per episode at $f_c{=}15$ Hz (2nd order, zero-phase filtfilt) to enforce temporal coherence. Random shooting picks each candidate independently, so the unfiltered $a^*$ trajectory is itself non-smooth despite being physics-aware. We call this the Bal-LP target distribution.

Velocity network and training

VelocityNet is an MLP[$61 \to 256 \to 256 \to 256 \to 12$] with SiLU activations, where the input concatenates $[x_t \,(12),\, t\,(1),\, s\,(48)]$ and the output is a 12-D velocity prediction. SiLU is chosen over ReLU because integrating a piecewise-linear field with hard corners produces erratic Euler trajectories — smoothness in the activation matches smoothness in the learned field. Training uses AdamW (lr$=10^{-3}$, wd$=10^{-5}$, cosine annealing) for 200 epochs at batch 512, with the CFM loss against OT-path targets. Total parameters: 150,540. Training takes ~30 s on an RTX 5070 Laptop. We train three flow models, one per PPO policy, on $a^*$ generated from that policy's rollouts.

Inference

At each control step we Euler-integrate the learned ODE from $x_0 = a_{\text{ppo}}$ for $N{=}20$ steps with $dt = t_{\text{end}}/N$. The smoothed action is $a_{\text{fm}} = x_N$. Per-step latency is ~6 ms, well within the 20 ms control budget at 50 Hz.

The 9-variant ablation

To isolate the contribution of each design axis we cross three reward levels with four post-hoc smoothing schemes: raw (no smoothing), +Flow (per-policy Bal-LP flow), +LP (causal IIR LP at 15 Hz), +Flow+LP (cascade). All variants are evaluated under identical 80-environment push-recovery tests with lateral impulses linearly spaced from 50–500 N, identical seeds, identical initial conditions.

The action-rate penalty has a sharp sweet spot at $\lambda{=}{-}0.01$: removing it weakens flow's benefit, pushing it extreme breaks the policy so badly that flow makes things worse.

Mechanism: why these numbers come out this way

Why some-reg pairs so well with flow. The $\lambda{=}{-}0.01$ penalty trains a policy that is already biased toward smooth-ish actions, but only weakly — the raw policy still falls 50% of the time. Crucially, this weak prior on smoothness produces rollout data where the random-shooting candidates around $a_{\text{ppo}}$ live in a region of action space that is reasonably well-behaved. When we generate optimal targets from these rollouts, the resulting $a^*$ distribution is both physics-aware and reachable from $a_{\text{ppo}}$ via short OT paths. The flow learns a high-quality velocity field, and at inference it confidently moves actions toward stable, smooth corrections.

Why no-reg gains less from flow. Without any reward-side smoothness signal, the no-reg policy is more aggressive and explores a wider, jerkier region of action space. Its raw fall rate is paradoxically lower (31.25%) because the policy has not been pushed away from rapid corrective actions — it falls less from natural pushes. But its rollout data is messier, the optimal targets that minimise our cost are further from $a_{\text{ppo}}$, and the OT paths the flow must learn are longer and less consistent. The flow still helps (−7.5 pp) but cannot match the some-reg gain.

Why extreme-reg + flow collapses. At $\lambda{=}{-}0.5$ the penalty dominates the reward; the policy converges to a near-static stance ($\bar v_x = -0.001$ m/s, action-rate RMS 0.049) — the robot literally does not walk. Optimal target generation runs on rollouts of an essentially stationary robot, producing an $a^*$ distribution in a tiny region of action space with no useful coverage of locomotion behaviour. The flow learns to push actions toward this near-stationary attractor, and during push tests the robot has no walking gait to recover with. Every push results in a fall.

Why Flow+LP underperforms Flow alone. The cascade no-reg + Flow + LP achieves the lowest HF spectral energy of any variant (1.23%) but a worse fall rate (37.5%) than no-reg + Flow alone (23.75%). The causal IIR filter at the end of the pipeline introduces phase lag exactly when responsiveness matters most — during a push. Smoothing past the point where corrections can land in time is actively harmful. This is the central failure mode of pure filtering: it cannot tell apart jitter from corrective signal, and aggressive smoothing kills both.

Smoothness vs jerk: a metric subtlety

HF spectral energy and action jerk (RMS of the discrete second derivative) measure related but distinct things. Spectral energy captures sustained oscillation power above 10 Hz; jerk captures point-to-point change in slope at every timestep. A signal can have low HF energy (no periodic ringing) while having high jerk (each step is a sharp transition to a new flat plateau). This is exactly what happens in our flow-smoothed actions: the flow removes oscillation but the discrete optimal targets it points to are not coupled across timesteps, so the resulting trajectory has clean spectra but is still piecewise-jumpy. Reducing jerk to a level matching extreme-reg's 0.07 RMS would require either much larger $\lambda_2$ in the optimal target cost or a temporal smoother on the flow output.

§ 05 · Reflections

What Worked, What Didn't, and the Process of Iteration

The path through five iterations

The first attempted target distribution was a per-joint adaptive low-pass filter applied directly to PPO actions (8–25 Hz cutoffs depending on which joints were noisiest). The flow trained on these targets achieved −19% HF energy and modest fall reduction (50% → 34%) but was unsatisfying: it just delegated low-pass filtering to a 150k-parameter network. Optimal target generation with stability-heavy cost weights ($\lambda_4{=}5$) produced targets that were physics-aware but yielded a robot that barely moved (4.55 m AB-race vs 5.62 m raw). A naive cascade — pipe the optimal flow through the LP flow at inference — gave 22% falls and 4.90 m, simply averaging the two failure modes. Rebalancing the cost weights to $\lambda_1{=}3,\, \lambda_4{=}2$ recovered speed but produced no smoothing benefit on its own. The key insight, arrived at last, was to combine the recipes at the target distribution level: generate targets with balanced weights, LP-filter them before training. The flow then learned a velocity field pointing toward "physics-aware and smooth" — a single objective baked into the data, not separated into two stages.

What didn't work

Inference cascading flow models trained independently on different target distributions does not work — the LP flow was trained expecting raw PPO actions as input but received already-corrected actions from the optimal flow, producing an unprincipled blend of both. Pure low-pass filtering at deployment is a poor general-purpose smoother: it is task-blind, introduces phase lag, and as the some-reg + LP variant shows, can actually increase fall rate by suppressing legitimate corrective actions. Pushing reward regularisation aggressively does not produce a better-behaved policy; it produces a non-functional one.

What we would change

A learned, state-dependent $t_{\text{end}}$ — applying full smoothing during steady-state walking and dialing back during disturbance recovery — would directly address the Flow+LP failure mode. An attention-based velocity network operating over joint tokens (rather than a flat MLP) could enforce inter-joint coordination explicitly, exploiting the kinematic tree. Sim-to-real evaluation on a physical Go1 is the most important missing piece — our smoothness gains are most valuable for hardware deployment, where actuator bandwidth and contact-model mismatch are the primary failure modes that smoother actions structurally mitigate.

Honest limitations

The headline 92% reduction in fall rate is real but comes paired with a 32% reduction in forward speed (5.62 m → 3.85 m AB-race), so the framing matters: this is jitter reduction trading speed for stability, not a free lunch. The $t_{\text{end}}$ knob lets operators sit anywhere on this Pareto front, but the front itself is non-trivial. The 5000-timestep training corpus is small; we have not characterised how the method scales with more data or harder tasks (rough terrain, payload variation). The optimal target generator uses random shooting with $K{=}16$ — a coarse search that almost certainly misses better candidates; gradient-based target optimisation or a larger candidate count would likely improve target quality. Finally, the flow is trained per-policy: we do not have a single flow that works across all three PPO checkpoints, and we have not tested whether one trained on some-reg rollouts transfers to no-reg deployment.
